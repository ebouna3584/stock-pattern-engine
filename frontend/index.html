Click any pattern to expand full details. Understanding these structures helps you interpret why the engine fires a signal — and what to expect next.

BULLISH
Ascending Triangle
Flat resistance + rising support — breakout expected above resistance
▼
Pattern Type
Bullish Continuation
Breakout Direction
Above flat resistance line
What to Look For
Price repeatedly tests the same resistance level while each pullback low is higher than the last. Two converging trendlines form a triangle pointing right.
Measured Move
Add the height of the triangle (from the first swing low to the resistance line) to the breakout point to get the price target.
Key signal: Buyers are getting more aggressive each dip (rising lows), but sellers keep defending the same resistance. Eventually buyers win and price bursts through. This is one of the most reliable continuation patterns.
BEARISH
Descending Triangle
Flat support + falling resistance — breakdown expected below support
▼
Pattern Type
Bearish Continuation
Breakout Direction
Below flat support line
What to Look For
Price keeps hitting the same support level while each rally high is lower than the last. Sellers are in control, buyers are weakening.
Measured Move
Subtract the triangle height from the breakdown point for the downside target.
Key signal: The mirror image of the ascending triangle. Each rally is weaker than the last — sellers are dominant. When buyers finally give up, price falls sharply below support.
NEUTRAL
Symmetrical Triangle
Both lines converge — breakout follows prior trend direction
▼
Pattern Type
Neutral Continuation
Breakout Direction
Follows prior macro trend
What to Look For
Lower highs and higher lows forming a coiling pattern toward an apex. Buyers and sellers are equally matched — a compression before expansion.
Measured Move
Same as other triangles — project the widest part of the triangle from the breakout point.
Key signal: Neither buyers nor sellers are winning. The standoff ends with a breakout — usually in the direction the stock was already moving before the triangle formed. Never trade against the prior trend on this pattern.
BULLISH
Bull Flag
Sharp pole up + small rectangular consolidation — continuation expected
▼
Pattern Type
Bullish Continuation
Breakout Direction
Above the flag's upper boundary
What to Look For
A fast, sharp upward move (the pole) followed by a mild downward or sideways consolidation forming a small parallel channel (the flag). Volume dries up during the flag.
Measured Move
Add the pole height to the breakout point from the flag's upper trendline.
Key signal: The pole represents strong momentum. The flag is a brief rest — traders taking profits — before the next leg up. Volume contracting during the flag and surging on breakout is the confirmation you want.
BEARISH
Bear Flag
Sharp pole down + small consolidation upward — continuation expected downward
▼
Pattern Type
Bearish Continuation
Breakout Direction
Below the flag's lower boundary
What to Look For
A sharp decline (the pole) followed by a mild upward consolidation. Sellers are resting before resuming the downtrend.
Measured Move
Subtract the pole height from the breakdown point below the flag's lower boundary.
Key signal: The upward consolidation is a "dead cat bounce" against the dominant downtrend. When the flag breaks down, the selling resumes with force.
CONTINUATION
Pennant
Like a flag but consolidation forms a small symmetrical triangle
▼
Pattern Type
Bullish or Bearish Continuation
Key Difference From Flag
The consolidation phase narrows into converging trendlines (a mini symmetrical triangle) rather than a parallel channel.
What to Look For
Strong pole move + small converging triangle consolidation. Volume contracts during the pennant, then expands sharply on breakout.
Measured Move
Same as flag — project the pole height from the breakout point of the pennant.
Key signal: Very similar psychology to a flag — brief consolidation after a strong move. The tighter, converging structure (vs. a channel) makes the breakout slightly more explosive statistically.
REVERSAL
Head & Shoulders
3 peaks (left shoulder < head > right shoulder) — sell on neckline break
▼
Pattern Type
Bearish Reversal
Confirmation
Close below the neckline (the line connecting the two troughs between the shoulders)
What to Look For
Three peaks: the middle peak (head) is the highest, the two side peaks (shoulders) are lower and roughly equal in height. Forms after a sustained uptrend.
Measured Move
Subtract the distance from the head to the neckline from the neckline breakout point.
Key signal: The pattern shows three attempts to push higher — only the second succeeds (the head). The third attempt (right shoulder) fails at a lower level, signaling buyers are exhausted. The neckline break is the entry trigger.
REVERSAL
Inverse Head & Shoulders
3 troughs — buy on neckline break, most reliable reversal pattern
▼
Pattern Type
Bullish Reversal (Most Reliable)
Confirmation
Close above the neckline with strong volume
What to Look For
Three troughs: the middle trough (head) is the lowest, the two outer troughs (shoulders) are higher. Forms after a sustained downtrend.
Measured Move
Add the distance from the head to the neckline to the neckline breakout point.
Key signal: Considered the single most reliable bullish reversal pattern in technical analysis. Three attempts by sellers to push lower — only the middle one succeeds. When the neckline breaks with volume, it typically signals a sustained upward move.
REVERSAL
Double Top / Double Bottom
2 equal highs = bearish reversal; 2 equal lows = bullish reversal
▼
Double Top
Bearish reversal. Two nearly equal highs (within ~3%) with a valley between them. Confirms on a close below the valley low.
Double Bottom
Bullish reversal. Two nearly equal lows with a peak between them. Confirms on a close above the peak.
Volume Pattern
Volume should be higher on the first peak/trough than the second. Declining volume on the second test shows conviction is fading.
Measured Move
The distance between the peaks and the valley, projected from the breakout level.
Key signal: The second test of a high or low is the key — if buyers (or sellers) can't push through the same level twice, the trend is likely over. The confirmation break of the valley/peak is the entry trigger.
BULLISH
Cup & Handle
U-shaped rounded base + small pullback handle — breakout above rim
▼
Pattern Type
Bullish Continuation
Formation Time
Usually 7 weeks to 65 weeks. Longer cups tend to produce more powerful breakouts.
What to Look For
A rounded, U-shaped decline and recovery (the cup) followed by a short sideways-to-down consolidation (the handle). The handle should not retrace more than 50% of the cup.
Measured Move
Add the depth of the cup to the breakout point (rim level). This gives the price target.
Key signal: The cup shows a long accumulation phase — institutional investors quietly building positions. The handle is a final shakeout of weak holders. The breakout above the rim (prior high) is the entry point. Popularized by William O'Neil's CAN SLIM system.
REVERSAL
Rising Wedge / Falling Wedge
Rising = bearish reversal; Falling = bullish reversal
▼
Rising Wedge (Bearish)
Both support and resistance lines slope upward but converge. Despite rising price, momentum is fading. Breakout expected downward below support.
Falling Wedge (Bullish)
Both lines slope downward but converge. Despite falling price, selling pressure is weakening. Breakout expected upward above resistance.
Key Distinction
The convergence is what matters. As the wedge narrows, energy builds for a sharp move in the opposite direction of the wedge slope.
Counter-intuitive
A rising wedge is bearish despite price going up. A falling wedge is bullish despite price going down. Always trade the breakout direction, not the wedge direction.
Key signal: Wedges are counter-intuitive — the slope of the wedge is the opposite direction of the expected breakout. Watch for RSI divergence (not confirming the price move) as an extra warning sign.
NEUTRAL
Rectangle / Channel
Price bouncing between flat support and resistance — breakout in trend direction
▼
Pattern Type
Consolidation / Neutral
Breakout Direction
Typically in the direction of the prior trend
What to Look For
Price oscillating between two flat (horizontal) lines — a ceiling (resistance) and a floor (support). At least 2–3 touches of each level.
Measured Move
Project the height of the rectangle (distance between support and resistance) from the breakout point.
Key signal: A rectangle is a patience pattern — the market is undecided. You can trade bounces within the range or wait for the breakout. The longer the rectangle, the more powerful the eventual breakout tends to be.

Technical indicators are mathematical calculations based on price and volume that help confirm patterns, measure momentum, and identify overbought/oversold conditions.

RSI — Relative Strength Index
Momentum oscillator (0–100)
Measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes. Calculated over 14 periods by default. Ranges from 0 to 100.
>70Overbought — potential sell signal. Doesn't mean sell immediately, but caution is warranted on new longs.
50–70Bullish territory — momentum is with the bulls.
30–50Bearish territory — momentum is with the bears.
<30Oversold — potential buy signal. Look for a turn upward as confirmation.
DivergencePrice makes a new high but RSI doesn't = bearish divergence (warning signal). Price makes a new low but RSI doesn't = bullish divergence.
MACD — Moving Average Convergence Divergence
Trend + momentum indicator
MACD Line = EMA(12) minus EMA(26). Signal Line = 9-period EMA of MACD. Histogram = MACD minus Signal.
BullishMACD crosses above Signal Line — momentum shifting upward.
BearishMACD crosses below Signal Line — momentum shifting downward.
HistogramGrowing histogram bars = strengthening momentum. Shrinking = momentum fading, potential reversal ahead.
Zero LineMACD crossing above zero = bullish trend change. Below zero = bearish trend change.
ATR — Average True Range
Volatility measure in dollar terms
Measures the average price range per bar (14 periods). Unlike RSI or MACD, ATR is not directional — it only measures how much a stock moves, not which direction.
High ATRMore volatile — wider stop losses needed. Use smaller position sizes to keep dollar risk constant.
Low ATRQuieter stock — tighter stops acceptable. Good for defined-risk setups.
Stop LossThis app sets stop loss at 1.5× ATR below entry. ATR-based stops adapt to each stock's specific volatility.
Bollinger Bands
Price vs. statistical range (20-SMA ± 2 std dev)
Three bands: middle band = 20-period SMA. Upper/lower bands = 2 standard deviations above/below. About 95% of price action occurs inside the bands.
Upper BandPrice touching or exceeding = potentially overbought. Not a sell signal alone — price can "walk the band" in strong trends.
Lower BandPrice at or below = potentially oversold. Look for a bounce confirmation before buying.
SqueezeBands narrowing = volatility contraction. Historically precedes a big move. Direction unknown — watch for the breakout.
EMA-20 / EMA-50
Exponential moving averages — short/medium trend
Exponential moving averages give more weight to recent prices. EMA-20 tracks short-term trend; EMA-50 tracks medium-term trend.
Above BothShort-term and medium-term trends are bullish — favorable conditions for longs.
Below BothDowntrend confirmed — avoid longs, look for short setups.
Golden CrossEMA-20 crosses above EMA-50 — bullish signal indicating a potential trend change upward.
Death CrossEMA-20 crosses below EMA-50 — bearish signal indicating downtrend is strengthening.
SMA-200
200-day simple moving average — long-term trend
The single most watched indicator by institutional investors worldwide. Represents approximately one full year of trading data. Price relationship to this line defines the macro trend.
Above SMA-200Long-term bull market territory. Most institutional buying happens here.
Below SMA-200Heightened risk. Even good technical setups have lower success rates below the 200-day.
Testing SMA-200A major support or resistance zone — watch for a decisive bounce or breakdown with volume.
Volume
Conviction behind price moves
Volume tells you how many shares (or contracts) changed hands. It's the most honest indicator — it shows what participants are actually doing with real money.
Rising Price + Rising VolumeStrong trend — institutional participation. High conviction.
Rising Price + Falling VolumeWeakening trend — potential reversal ahead. Fewer participants buying.
Breakout + Volume SpikeVolume 2–3× average on breakout = institutional participation. High confidence in the move.
Confidence Score (0–100)
This app's proprietary pattern quality score
A composite score combining trendline R² quality, volume confirmation, RSI/MACD indicator alignment, and how cleanly the price action fits the geometric pattern.
70–100Strong pattern geometry — high-conviction setup. Multiple factors aligned.
50–69Moderate confidence — pattern is valid but some conditions are borderline.
Below 50Low conviction — treat as speculative. Consider waiting for more confirmation or skip the trade.

Every trade plan generated by this app contains these fields. Here's exactly what each one means and why it's calculated the way it is.

Entry Price
Where to enter the trade
The recommended price to enter the trade. Based on the breakout level — you enter when price actually breaks through the pattern boundary, not before. Entering before the breakout is called "anticipating" — it might work occasionally but leads to many false entries. The breakout level is calculated from the trendlines of the detected pattern.
Stop Loss
Where to exit if wrong
The price where you admit the trade failed and exit. Calculated as 1.5× ATR below the entry price (for long trades). ATR-based stops adapt to each stock's specific volatility — wider for volatile stocks, tighter for quiet ones. This prevents getting stopped out by normal price fluctuations while still limiting losses if the trade genuinely fails. Never move your stop loss further away from entry once in a trade.
Take Profit 1 (TP-1)
Conservative first target
The first, conservative profit target — approximately 1× ATR above the entry price. When TP-1 is reached, the recommended strategy is to take 30–40% of your position off. This locks in real gains and reduces emotional pressure on the remaining position. The remaining shares continue riding toward TP-2 and TP-3.
Take Profit 2 (TP-2)
Measured move target
The measured move target — based on the pattern height projected from the breakout point. This is the classic technical analysis target derived from the pattern geometry. For example, if an ascending triangle is 5 points tall and breaks out at $100, TP-2 would be approximately $105. Take another 30–40% of the original position here.
Take Profit 3 (TP-3)
Extended target
The extended target — approximately 3× ATR from entry, or the full pattern projection depending on which is larger. This is for the final 20–30% of the position. Let this portion run as long as the trend continues. Only exit here if price shows signs of reversal or if a new opposing pattern forms.
Risk/Reward Ratio
Return per dollar risked
Calculated as (TP-1 minus Entry) ÷ (Entry minus Stop Loss). A 2:1 ratio means you make $2 for every $1 you risk. This is the minimum threshold for most professional traders. Why? Because even with a 40% win rate, a 2:1 R/R means you're profitable. Never take a trade below 1.5:1 — the math doesn't work in your favor.
Position Size %
Portfolio allocation
What percentage of your total portfolio to allocate to this trade. Based on the "2% rule" — risk no more than 2% of total capital per trade, divided by the ATR stop distance. Example: $10,000 portfolio, 2% risk = $200 max loss per trade. If stop is $2 away, position size = 100 shares. This keeps any single loss from being catastrophic.
Risk Score
LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH
A composite risk score combining: current volatility (ATR relative to price), RSI extremity (how overbought/oversold), distance from breakout (how extended is the move), MACD alignment (is momentum confirming?), and volume surge (is volume supporting the move?). LOW = favorable conditions. HIGH = multiple warning signs — reduce position size or skip.
Signal
BUY / SELL / WATCH
BUY: Bullish pattern detected with breakout alignment — conditions favor a long entry. SELL: Bearish pattern detected — conditions favor a short entry or exiting long positions. WATCH: Pattern is detected but no confirmed breakout yet. Add to your watchlist. Wait for a closing price above resistance (or below support) with above-average volume before acting.

Technical patterns don't exist in a vacuum. Understanding what's driving the broader market helps you assess whether a pattern is likely to succeed or fail. Here's how major macro events affect different sectors.

📈 Interest Rate Hikes
The Fed raises rates to fight inflation. Borrowing costs rise across the economy. Growth stocks fall hardest because higher discount rates reduce the present value of future cash flows.
Banks + Financials + Tech − Biotech − REITs − Bonds fall
📉 Interest Rate Cuts
The Fed cuts rates to stimulate the economy. Cheaper borrowing fuels growth. Growth stocks rally as future earnings become more valuable. The dollar typically weakens.
Tech/Growth + Real Estate + Consumer Discretionary + Banks −
🔥 Inflation Rising
Real assets outperform. Energy, commodities, and materials protect purchasing power. High-growth tech suffers as discount rates rise and valuations compress.
Energy + Gold + Materials + Consumer Staples (neutral) Tech −
❄️ Inflation Falling (Disinflation)
Growth stocks recover as discount rates ease. Bond prices rise as yields drop. Consumer confidence improves, benefiting discretionary spending.
Tech/Growth + Bonds recover Consumer Discretionary +
💪 Strong Jobs Report
Signals a healthy economy — but also raises expectations for rate hikes. Dollar strengthens. Short-term: tech may fall on rate fears. Financial sector benefits from stronger economic activity.
Financials + USD + Tech volatile
😟 Weak Jobs Report
Raises expectations for rate cuts. Growth stocks rally on the prospect of easier monetary conditions. The market often "bad news is good news" when the Fed is expected to respond.
Tech/Growth + Rate-sensitive sectors +
⚔️ Geopolitical Tension / War
Triggers risk-off mode. Investors flee to safe havens. Defense spending surges. Oil spikes on supply disruption fears. Travel and tourism collapse immediately.
Defense + Gold + Oil spikes USD/Treasuries + Airlines − Travel −
😰 Recession Fears
Investors rotate to defensive sectors that provide stable earnings regardless of economic conditions. Cyclical sectors (tied to economic growth) get hit hardest.
Healthcare + Utilities + Consumer Staples + Gold + Auto/Retail − Industrials −
🎯 Strong Earnings Season
Broad market confidence rises. Individual stocks beating expectations become reliable technical breakouts — fundamentals and technicals align. Sector leaders attract capital flows.
Earnings beaters surge Sector leaders + Earnings misses punished
🔄 Fed Pivot
One of the biggest market catalysts. A pivot from hiking to cutting rates triggers a broad market rally — especially tech and growth. A pivot from cutting to hiking causes a broad selloff.
Hike→Cut Pivot: Tech + Broad Market + Cut→Hike Pivot: Broad selloff
💵 Dollar Strengthening
A stronger dollar pressures commodities (priced in USD) and emerging markets (whose debt is often dollar-denominated). US multinationals with large foreign revenues see earnings compressed.
US Importers + Commodities − Emerging Markets − US Multinationals −
💸 Dollar Weakening
A weaker dollar boosts commodity prices and makes US exports more competitive. Emerging markets rally as their currencies gain relative strength and dollar-denominated debt becomes easier to service.
US Exporters + Gold + Commodities + Emerging Markets +

These principles separate consistently profitable traders from those who lose money. None of them are complicated — the difficulty is in applying them consistently, especially when emotions are running high.

1
The 1–2% Rule. Never risk more than 1–2% of your total portfolio on a single trade. Position sizing is more important than entry timing. Even 10 consecutive losses only costs 10–20% of capital.
2
Wait for the breakout. Don't anticipate. Enter after price actually closes above resistance (or below support) — not before. Most failed trades come from entering too early on hope rather than confirmation.
3
Volume confirms everything. A breakout on weak volume has a 40–50% higher chance of failure. Wait for volume 1.5–2× the 20-bar average on the breakout candle before committing.
4
Check the broader market first. Even the best individual setup fails in a strong bear market. Know whether the S&P 500 is above or below its 200-day SMA before taking any new long positions.
5
Multi-timeframe confirmation. If the daily chart shows an ascending triangle AND the weekly chart shows an uptrend, the setup is significantly stronger. Never fight the higher timeframe trend.
6
Earnings can destroy any pattern instantly. Always check if an earnings announcement falls within your planned holding period. The best technical setup becomes worthless if the company misses earnings.
7
Confidence Score above 70. Patterns scoring above 70 have strong geometry — multiple conditions are aligned. Below 50, treat it as low conviction and either reduce size or skip entirely.
8
The 2:1 Rule. A Risk/Reward ratio below 2:1 is generally not worth taking. One losing trade wipes out two winners. The math only works in your favor at 2:1 or better.
9
Diversify across sectors. Don't concentrate all capital in tech or any single industry. When a sector gets hit by macro headwinds, diversification is the only protection.
10
Cut losses fast, let winners run. Most consistently profitable traders have win rates of only 40–50%. They profit because their winners are 2–3× bigger than their losers. This requires the emotional discipline to cut losses before they grow.
11
The 200-day SMA matters most. The most watched line by institutional investors worldwide. Price above it = bull market territory. Below it = heightened caution on all long positions.
12
Keep a trade journal. Record every entry, exit, the rationale, and what actually happened. Review it monthly. Professional traders attribute 30–40% of their improvement to journaling — it's the feedback loop that creates growth.
13
Pattern reliability ranking (highest to lowest): Inverse H&S > Ascending Triangle > Cup & Handle > Double Bottom > Head & Shoulders > Falling Wedge > Symmetrical Triangle. Weight your position sizing accordingly.
14
Multiple patterns on the same stock. When two or more patterns align simultaneously (rare), it's a high-conviction setup worth larger sizing — assuming all other conditions (volume, indicators, market environment) also align.
